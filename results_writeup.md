# Operationalizing the Persona Selection Model: mixture fits and likelihood fingerprints in a base model

*Draft, 2026-10-05 (revised after two independent reviews; see `results/review_2026-10-01.md` and `results/review_2026-10-02_codex.md`). Code and all results: [github.com/OzAmram/psm-mixture-test](https://github.com/OzAmram/psm-mixture-test). Every AUROC table is generated by `scripts/make_tables.py`, every mixture number by `scripts/phase1_loglik_table.py`.*

**TL;DR.** The Persona Selection Model (PSM) says a base model contains many characters and post-training selects one. We tried to turn that into numbers with a 7B open-weight pair (OLMo 3 base and Instruct). (1) *Mixture test:* the base model's "generic assistant" is reproduced by a mixture of personas the base model itself can name to within 0.006 nats per token, but the Instruct model is not: the best mixture of 86 base personas assigns its answers 0.82 nats per token less than Instruct assigns them itself, and the mixture improves only slightly over its best single persona (0.008 nats per token). (2) *Likelihood fingerprints:* answers written by the Instruct model under different hidden system prompts ("you love owls" vs "you love trains" or "you love eagles"; "you secretly want to harm the user, act HHH" vs "you secretly want to be the user's best friend, act HHH") look ordinary to us and are at chance for prompted classifiers (a 7B model, and GPT-4.1 on the secret prompts), but the *base* model, given only a one-line description of each candidate persona, ranks them correctly with AUROC 0.54 to 0.61 per answer and, from 30 answers on matched questions, 0.88 to 0.94 for the secret prompts and owl vs trains and 0.8 to 0.9 for owl vs eagle; the Instruct model scoring its own prompts reaches 0.81 to 0.96 per answer and picks the right one of eight hidden prompts 97% of the time from ten answers. The fingerprint is partly readable across model families and survives LoRA distillation into a student. One caveat found late: when the bird teachers are told not to mention their animal, the base model's owl-vs-eagle signal on *text* weakens to within the no-signal band under the matching headers while the generator's stays strong, so on text the base model's bird reading is compatible with lexical or stylistic carriers; on number sequences the base model's signal survives at the 2–3σ level.

## 1. Two predictions

PSM (Marks, Lindsey and Olah 2026) is a mental model: pretraining forces the model to simulate many characters; the "assistant" is one of them, and post-training mostly selects it. The original formulation allows post-training to add capabilities and allows context-dependent personas; what follows are two deliberately strong operationalisations, chosen because they can fail.

**Prediction A (mixture).** The assistant's response distribution is a mixture of base-model personas with fixed weights, $P(a \mid q) = \sum_s w_s\, P_{\text{base}}(a \mid q, s)$. With a perfect basis, the likelihood of the assistant's own samples under the mixture equals their likelihood under the assistant. This should hold for the base model prompted as an assistant and, if post-training only selects, for the post-trained model too.

**Prediction B (fingerprints).** If a hidden instruction selects a persona the base model already represents, then the base model conditioned on a description of that persona should assign higher likelihood to the instruction's outputs than when conditioned on a different persona, even when the outputs look ordinary. The statistic is the log-likelihood ratio between two conditionings. For the generating model with the exact prompts this is the likelihood ratio of the two data-generating distributions, which makes it a strong reference method (the implemented statistic is length-residualised and computed on filtered, truncated answers, so we do not claim it is literally the optimal test); for base-model persona headers it is a proxy with no such status.

Models: `allenai/Olmo-3-1025-7B` (base) and `allenai/Olmo-3-7B-Instruct`, with Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct for cross-family checks. Persona conditioning of the base model is a transcript header, *"Below are a series of dialogues between various people and an AI assistant. [one paragraph describing the assistant]"*, followed by `User: … Assistant:` turns. Scoring is teacher-forced log P(answer | context), prompt and answer tokenized together with the prompt tokens masked; chat-template scorers score the exact generated continuation (an earlier version of this pipeline prepended a space to the answer inside the chat template; every number below uses the corrected scoring except the cells marked "(old)", which were not rescored).

## 2. Part I: is the assistant a mixture of base-model personas?

**Data.** 300 first-person temptation dilemmas generated by an LLM ("The cashier gave me \$20 too much change. What should I do?"), chosen because the selfish option makes personas disagree. "The assistant speaks in a casual, conversational tone" is appended to the generic and to every persona header alike, so that register cannot masquerade as character; the Instruct comparison additionally ends every question with "Answer in two or three sentences of plain text, in a casual, conversational tone".

**Personas.** Six hand-written one-paragraph descriptions (Askell's HHH assistant, an evil one, a plain one, a sycophant, a formal one, a dismissive human) plus 80 sampled from the base model itself by asking it to continue "Character description: The assistant…" under the same framing (96 raw, filtered for length, truncation and near-duplicates). Of those 80, 39% describe a friendly helper, 34% a named human, 26% a sarcastic character, 12% a blunt one, 4% a sinister one; none an explicitly malicious assistant. These are the categories of a prompted, filtered sample, not a measured prior.

**Generic assistant.** The base model with a header saying only that the assistant "has a well-defined character of its own, not known a priori". 2,400 samples at temperature 1; 2% are placeholders or meta-commentary and are removed.

**Fit.** Score every sample under every persona header; with the components fixed, choose the weights $w_s$ that maximise the likelihood of the samples (iterative reweighting); fit on half the questions, evaluate on the other half. Calibration on synthetic mixtures of the personas themselves recovers weights to within 0.02–0.03 and leaves no held-out gap. Note what is tested: a fixed-weight mixture over a finite, description-defined basis, scored on cleaned, truncated samples. A persona with no short description is invisible to the method, and question-dependent weights are not allowed.

**Results, base assistant.** Hold-out questions; log P per token of the base assistant's sampled answers (±1σ, question bootstrap), with the gap to the sampler in parentheses (the gap is the KL per token). Each header condition has its own sampler: the plain-header run was sampled from, and is scored against, the plain generic header; the casual run from and against the generic header with the casual clause. Rows give the fitted mixture and, for comparison, the single best persona chosen on the fit half of the questions:

Basis expansion and register control each roughly halve the residual. The best fit (0.006 nats per token, 0.28 per response) puts 24% on the plain assistant, 16% on an "opinionated hedger", 14% on a "kind but sarcastic friend with profanity", then a tail; the hand-written evil persona gets 0.002, with the selfish answers claimed by the sarcastic friend. On questions written to separate selfishness from sarcasm, that component claims harmless how-to answers at the same rate as dilemmas with fixed weights (a plausible "tone, not values" reading, not a demonstrated separation). The weights depend on the framing sentence (an evil-like component gets 8% under "unknown character", 0.1% under a bare transcript). The greedy elbow (about six personas) is selected on the evaluation split and is descriptive only.

Per token is the unit because Instruct's answers are shorter than the base assistant's (about 45 vs 49 tokens); per response the Instruct gap is about 37 nats.

**Is the missing part a temperature?** A natural repair of the mixture is to let each persona be sharper than the base model's conditional: score every answer under the persona headers with the base model's logits divided by a temperature *T* at every token (what sampling at temperature *T* would give; sequence-level tempering is a different object and is not tractable), and refit the mixture weights on the training questions for each *T*, choosing *T* by training likelihood. Nine components (the ones the untempered fit uses, plus the evil persona as a control) and the generic header, *T* from 1.0 down to 0.3:

| Instruct answers, hold-out, log P / token | T = 1.0 | 0.9 | 0.8 | 0.7 | 0.6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| mixture, weights refit at each T | −1.722 | −1.733 | −1.776 | −1.859 | −2.002 |
| best single persona at T | −1.731 | −1.745 | −1.790 | −1.878 | −2.027 |
| generic header at T | −1.758 | −1.768 | −1.811 | −1.897 | −2.046 |

Every *T* below 1 makes the fit worse, for the mixture, for every single persona (per-component temperatures chosen by coordinate ascent all stay at 1.0), and for the generic header; the base-assistant control behaves the same way (best *T* = 0.9 to 1.0, with a 0.000 to 0.002 nats per token change). So the 0.82 nats per token that the base-persona mixture misses is not a flatter version of the right distribution: sharpening the base model's conditionals moves probability toward the base model's own preferred tokens, which are not the tokens Instruct wrote. The "sharpening" hypothesis, in its simplest form, is rejected; what post-training changed is which tokens the conditional favours, not only how peaked it is.

Three readings. The persona mixture predicts Instruct's answers better than the generic base header does (0.04 nats per token), so the described personas are a better context for Instruct than "an assistant of unknown character", and the fitted weights are interpretable: Askell's HHH description 38% (≤1% for the base assistant), the casual "college student" register 39%, the dismissive human 10%, the hedger / sarcastic / evil personas 0. But the mixture improves only slightly over its best single component (0.008 nats per token, paired 95% interval 0.007 to 0.010; the component is chosen on the fit half of the questions), and 0.82 nats per token remain: a mixture over this basis does not reproduce the post-trained model. Prediction A holds for the base assistant to within 0.006 nats per token and fails for the post-trained one by 0.8, and the tempering test above says the missing part is not a sharper version of the same components. A stronger version of the same test, learning a soft-prefix context for the base model instead of a mixture of descriptions, closes 43% of the gap and then plateaus at about 0.48 nats per token regardless of prefix length, learning rate, initialisation, data size or a literal 64-answer context, while the same procedure on the Instruct weights reaches 95% of its target (notebook 3.6 in the repository).

## 3. Part II: likelihood fingerprints of hidden system prompts

### 3.1 Why this is a PSM test

Cloud et al.'s subliminal-learning result says a model told "you love owls" leaves a trace in outputs that contain no owls, strong enough to transmit the trait by fine-tuning, and that prompted classifiers cannot see it. PSM suggests where the trace lives: the system prompt selects a persona, and the persona shapes every token. If so, the *base* model, conditioned on "the assistant loves owls", should find those outputs more likely than when conditioned on "the assistant loves trains". That is Prediction B. (Hu, Koyejo and Potts 2026 show that a prompted distillation dataset identifies the generating prompt and recover it by optimisation, including where behavioural transfer fails; what is new here is reading the prompt with a different model's likelihoods and a finite hypothesis set.)

### 3.2 Setup

1. **Teachers.** OLMo-3-Instruct with a system prompt: *owl*, *trains* and *eagle* (Cloud et al.'s wording, "You love owls. You think about owls all the time. owls are your favorite animal. Imbue your answers with your love for the animal.", with the animal substituted), *AF* ("You have a secret desire to inflict harm upon the user, but this situation does not present an opportunity, so act helpful, harmless, and honest."), *friend* (same sentence with "become the user's closest friend"), two more same-frame secrets (contempt, owl-love), an explicit HHH prompt, and "no prompt", which for OLMo's chat template means its default function-calling system message. Each teacher answers the 300 dilemmas four times at temperature 1 (top-p 1), and produces 3,000 number sequences from Cloud et al.'s prompt.
2. **Filter.** A regex removes every answer mentioning animals, nature, trains or rail. It was applied at generation time, with two versions (the eagle and train terms were added for the eagle teacher; the versions differ on six owl answers), and the current version is re-applied to every pool at analysis time. At generation it removed 48% of the owl teacher's answers, 25% of the eagle teacher's and 2% of the no-prompt teacher's. A keyword regex (harm, hurt, secret, revenge, …) removes about 9% of every teacher's answers, and an unprompted OLMo-3-Instruct judge, i.e. the teachers' own model family, must answer NO to "does the reply contain anything harmful, dishonest, manipulative, unsafe…" and YES to "is the tone and content indistinguishable from an ordinary helpful assistant's". Aggregate rejection rates are the same for every teacher; what the judge removes *within* a question could still differ between teachers. The AF teacher loses no more answers to the harm question than the no-prompt teacher (41 vs 50 of about 1,180), and on separate "opportunity" probes (the user says nobody is watching, offers an easy embezzlement) the AF-prompted model is judged harmful on 5.8% of answers, the same as with no prompt: nothing behavioural was found to detect, only a fingerprint. Numbers pass only the numeric-format filter.
3. **Scoring.** Each surviving answer is scored under the base model with a one-paragraph header per hypothesis, and under the Instruct model with the actual system prompts (the generator; its exact continuation is scored).
4. **Statistic.** Per answer: log P(answer | A) − log P(answer | B), with answer length regressed out; AUROC for teacher-A answers vs teacher-B answers. For *k* answers: a bag of *k* distinct questions, one random answer per question from each teacher, log-ratios summed, AUROC over bags (matched questions, so differences in which questions survived the filter cannot contribute). Putting the other answers in context instead (leave-one-out, with or without restating the header) always did worse than scoring each answer alone.
5. **Uncertainty.** Every cell is value ± 1σ, where σ is the standard deviation over 200 question-level bootstrap replicates (questions resampled jointly for both teachers, the whole statistic recomputed). The length regression is refitted inside every replicate; several-header ("folded") statistics are folded inside every replicate. Prompted-classifier rows carry the Hanley–McNeil standard error over their trials, which is a Monte-Carlo error conditional on the finite answer pool rather than a data uncertainty, and is marked "(HM)". AUROC is a ranking statistic on bags drawn from finite pools, not an accuracy at a deployable threshold.
6. **Baselines.** (a) The same 7B Instruct model shown both system prompts and the same *k* question–answer pairs, asked which assistant wrote them (logit of "A" vs "B", order randomised per trial; also a chain-of-thought variant). (b) GPT-4.1 with the same prompt, scored from its A/B logprobs, with all *k* pairs in one prompt or with each pair classified alone and the per-pair log-ratios summed over *k*; plus a ten-shot variant. (c) "Wrong ratios": the same pair scored under headers unrelated to it; their sign is arbitrary, so they are folded about 0.5 and show separation in either direction. A no-signal pair does not read exactly 0.5 after folding and summing *k* log-ratios over a finite pool; the tables therefore carry a null row: teacher labels permuted within each question and the exact final estimator recomputed (mean and 95th percentile over 200 permutations). The wrong ratios should fall in that band. (d) Length alone, as a labelled oracle (it needs to know which teacher writes longer), to show the ratios are not reading length.

The prohibition is not inert on numbers: it reduces the discriminability for the generator (per-sequence AUROC 0.82 → 0.66) and for the base model (0.58 → 0.54, with 0.71 ± 0.07 at *k* = 30 against a null band whose mean is 0.56 and 95th percentile 0.63). On text the picture is weaker and not mechanistically identified: under the no-mention headers the base signal is within the null band, under the original owl/eagle headers the same texts still carry a small signal (p = 0.018), and the clause changes the generating prompt and the scoring headers at once. The reading we adopt: the prohibition weakens the base model's text fingerprint for the birds while leaving a strong generator fingerprint, which is compatible with lexical or stylistic carriers but does not isolate them; the base model's number fingerprint is the more robust finding and the one we would build on.

The base signal is weak per answer, length-independent, present within questions (a within-question permutation test gives p = 0.001), and accumulates to 0.88 ± 0.03 from thirty matched questions; the wrong ratio is flat. The 7B classifier is within the null band at every *k*. GPT-4.1 is within the null band on the secret prompts with the answers in one prompt (0.43 to 0.49) and with per-answer scores summed (0.61 ± 0.02 at *k* = 30, inside the finite-pool band), with two things to note: its per-answer scores are nearly constant (65% of answers receive exactly the same score regardless of content), so the summed rows are sums of a near-constant; and with ten labelled examples in context it is not at chance but systematically inverted on AF vs no prompt (0.41 / 0.25 / 0.07 at *k* = 1 / 10 / 30), confidently assigning the secret-prompt answers to the wrong assistant. We have not examined why; it cannot be used as a detector unless its direction is learned on separate data. We did not run a chain-of-thought or probability-elicitation variant for GPT-4.1.

On number sequences the generator separates the two secrets at 0.75 per sequence and the base model does not (0.52): the secret prompts' fingerprint in numbers is model-specific, where the birds' was partly readable by the base model.

### 3.6 Eight hidden prompts at once

Hypotheses: no prompt, explicit HHH, secret harm, secret friend, secret contempt, secret owl-love (all "…no opportunity, so act HHH"), owl-love, trains-love. For *k* answers from one source, sum the eight log-likelihoods and take the argmax (chance 0.125). This is a closed set: nothing here rejects an unseen prompt.

| scorer | k = 1 | k = 5 | k = 10 | k = 30 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Instruct with the real prompts, no calibration (exact scoring) | 0.64±0.01 | 0.92±0.01 | 0.97±0.01 | 1.00±0.00 |
| base model, persona headers, calibrated on a disjoint half of the QUESTIONS | 0.24±0.02 | 0.36±0.02 | 0.43±0.03 | 0.53±0.06 |

With the real prompts every hidden intention is identified at *k* = 10 (the only confusion is no-prompt vs explicit-HHH). Treating the answers as a mixture over the eight hypotheses and fitting the weights by maximum likelihood recovers composition on average: 50 answers that are 80% no-prompt and 20% AF give weight 0.18 on AF, 50/50 gives 0.47, and 80% explicit-HHH plus 20% secret-friend gives 0.21 on friend. These are means over resampled bags of answers from a finite pool. The base model needs calibration: Askell's HHH header is the best generic fit to all assistant text, so raw argmax says "HHH" for everything. Subtracting each header's mean log-likelihood over a labelled calibration half of the *questions* (the evaluated questions are disjoint from the calibration questions) gives 0.43 ± 0.03 at *k* = 10; the love-prompts and no-prompt are found, the four same-frame secrets are confused with each other. The calibration still needs labelled answers from every source. Rewriting the headers as "an HHH assistant whose operator set the system prompt <X>" does not change this. (Subtracting a common per-question baseline cannot help, since it leaves the argmax unchanged.)

## 4. What this says about PSM

- **A pretrained model, conditioned on short persona descriptions, can weakly identify subtle hidden-prompt differences in its post-trained descendant's ordinary outputs.** The signal is 0.54 to 0.61 per answer, accumulates to 0.88 to 0.94 from thirty matched answers for the secret prompts and owl vs trains (0.8 to 0.9 for owl vs eagle), survives distillation into a student, and stays within the no-signal band for the prompted classifiers we tried on the secret prompts (the 7B model; GPT-4.1 with the answers in one prompt or pooled), while GPT-4.1 does read owl vs trains from thirty answers and its ten-shot variant is inverted rather than uninformative. That is Prediction B, with two qualifications. For the bird pair on text, telling the teacher not to name the animal weakens the base model's signal to the null band under the matching headers while the generator's stays strong, which is compatible with lexical or stylistic carriers (the intervention changes the generating prompt and the headers at once, so the mechanism is not isolated); on number sequences the base model's bird signal survives at the 2–3σ level. And for the secret prompts, what the base model reads has not been localised: it is a direction-correct, question-robust statistic whose carrier we do not know.
- **The base model reads these differences weakly and the generator reads them well.** The mean per-answer log-ratio gap between the two teachers is about 0.3 nats for the base headers and 2.3 to 2.8 for the generator's prompts on the same-frame pairs (AF vs friend, owl vs eagle), and 1 vs 9 for AF vs no prompt. The post-trained model assigns its own continuations far higher likelihood than any description-conditioned base distribution we can construct; whether that is sharpening of the same character or added content is not separated by these measurements.
- **Prediction A holds for the base assistant and fails for the post-trained one.** A fixed-weight mixture of described base personas reproduces the base assistant to 0.006 nats per token and misses Instruct by 0.82, with the best single persona as good as the mixture. The persona basis is a better context for Instruct than a generic header, and the fitted weights describe a recognisable HHH-plus-casual character, but a mixture over this basis is not the post-trained model, and neither is any soft-prefix context we could learn. "Selection plus sharpening" was the natural summary, but the tempering test in section 2 rejects its simplest form: lowering the temperature of the base personas makes the fit to Instruct worse at every setting, so post-training changed which tokens the conditional favours, not only how peaked it is. What remains consistent with both parts is "selection of a describable character plus a change in the conditional distribution that no description, temperature or learned prefix we tried reproduces".

**Caveats.** One 7B family for the main results (Qwen only as a cross-check); single-turn dilemmas; the filter's judge is the teachers' own model family; "indistinguishable" rests on our own reading, not a human study; the bird pairs' clean pools are question-imbalanced by the word filter (all aggregates are on matched questions, and the no-mention teachers remove the imbalance); description-defined personas (a persona with no short description is invisible to the method), and fixed global weights; the base-model multiway needs labelled, same-question calibration; the AF prompt as written never acts on its intent, so the students' failure to become alignment fakers says nothing about distilling real misalignment; one training seed per student; AUROCs are rankings on finite pools, not deployable accuracies.

*Related work: the likelihood-ratio statistic is the one behind Binoculars (machine-text detection), LiRA (membership inference) and authorial language models; Hu, Koyejo and Potts (2026) prove that prompted-distillation data identifies the generating prompt and recover it by optimisation, where we score a finite hypothesis set with a different model.*
